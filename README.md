# BattleCraft Server Pack

BattleCraft is a consolidated Minecraft Forge 1.20.1 server-oriented modpack that integrates multiple custom gameplay modules into a unified codebase.

## Technical Specifications

*   **Version**: dated releases — `2026.9.13-alpha` in files, `13.09.26 ALPHA` on screen. This is a public test build: expect rough edges and report what breaks.
*   **Platform**: Minecraft Forge 1.20.1 (Forge 47.4.22)
*   **Java Version**: Toolchain set to Java 17
*   **Build System**: Gradle

## Interface

Every HUD element in the pack is drawn by a single vector renderer — no textures, one palette, one corner radius, one animation model. Every shape is filled through one closed-path pass with an alpha-feathered edge, so panels, bars, dots and icons stay antialiased instead of showing stair-stepped pixels at any GUI scale. All motion uses frame-rate independent exponential smoothing, so transitions stay smooth at any FPS.

Surfaces are built from a single glass material, and it is kept to one layer on purpose. The frame is captured before the HUD is drawn, reduced to a quarter of its size and put through several widening blur passes, and each panel samples that; what shows through is a frosted wash of the scene rather than a readable image, and because the blur is a real kernel rather than a coarse mip level the wash stays smooth instead of breaking into blocks. Along the border the sample position is displaced so the content compresses the way it does through the thickness of real glass, and the red and blue channels are displaced by slightly different amounts, so the rim carries the faint colour fringe a thick lens gives you. Which way it goes is a knob: pulled inward the edge stretches the panel's own content outward, pushed outward it drags in the frame from beyond the window the way a bevelled edge does, and a second knob sets how far the sample travels, measured in edge thicknesses rather than in screen height, so the strength does not change with the window size. In menus the capture happens after the screen has dimmed the world, so a panel is always a shade darker than the surface it sits on instead of glowing with a brighter copy of the frame behind it. Over that sits one vertical body gradient — no inset highlight plates, no painted outline — dark and dense enough that a panel always reads a shade below whatever it covers, the way frosted glass does. Depth comes from that gradient, the edge lensing and a hairline of light along the top edge. The captured frame is forced opaque before it is blurred: the game clears the sky to zero alpha and draws the sun, the moon and the stars straight over it, so without that pass a panel held against the sky sampled nothing and simply disappeared. Refraction can be switched off in the config; the material then falls back to the opaque gradient. On the first launch the player is asked once which of the three finishes the interface should use — the full liquid glass, clear panels over a blurred capture, or clear panels with no capture at all — and the answer is written next to the instance and never asked again. The middle mode drops the lens rings and the colour fringe, the last one skips the capture entirely, so a weak machine pays for a gradient and nothing else. The capture itself is taken once per frame no matter how many surfaces ask for it, and the letter halo drops its diagonal passes at sizes where they cannot be seen.

*   **Island**: a pill in the top-left corner, above the notifications, which stack under it and follow its height as it grows and shrinks. At rest it carries a square avatar with rounded corners - the cover of whatever is playing, else the player's Discord picture, else the head off their own skin, drawn as a plain square: the head and hat layers are read back off the skin texture once, composed, and re-cut whenever the drawn size changes, at a side that is a whole multiple of eight screen pixels and laid on the physical pixel grid, so every skin pixel keeps the same width and the edges stay straight - their name, and the frame rate and round-trip to the server spelled out as `fps` and `ping` rather than glyphs, both sampled once every twenty ticks rather than every frame, the latency shifting from the text tone through amber to red as it climbs. When audio starts anywhere on the machine the island springs open into a card: artwork, track, artist beneath it, a progress bar and the elapsed and total time set as one line under it. Every word on the island is set in the live ink rather than the dimmed or faint one - artist, times and the FPS and ping units included - because a pill that small sits over the world rather than over a panel, and the quieter inks were read there as a rendering fault rather than a hierarchy. That bar is not driven straight off the reported position: ordinary playback is the clock running, so the position the session reports is checked against the position the clock predicts, and only a disagreement larger than a second is treated as someone having moved the track. When it is, the fill travels to the new place over just under half a second instead of jumping there, and a lit head rides the edge of it while it travels and is gone once it arrives, since a fill crossing a three-minute track is otherwise too small a movement to read. The clock beside it goes straight to the new time, because a number that eases is a number that is wrong for half a second. A change of track resets the bar rather than sending it travelling. After five seconds it folds back to a pill laid out in two rows: the title and a sound visualiser beside it on top - and that visualiser is a real meter rather than a loop. Each of its six bars is a band of the spectrum rather than a share of one number: the bridge opens a loopback capture, keeps the last 1024 samples in a ring, windows them and runs an FFT forty times a second, then folds the bins into six bands spaced by octave, so bass, voice and cymbals move independently because they are independent. The capture is aimed at the process tree of whatever application the session names, so the bars answer that player and nothing else the machine is doing - a voice in a Discord call, a video in another window, the interface's own clicks - and it follows the player from one application to the next, tearing the old capture down and raising a new one on the tree the session moved to. The root of that tree is found through the process table rather than guessed, because a browser plays its audio out of a child process. Only when no application is known at all does it fall back to the older capture, which takes everything except the game's own process tree, and a machine where neither can open falls back again to a plain device loopback. It reads the application's stream before the system volume control, so the bars stand the same height whether the machine is at full volume or a quarter of it, and the player's own volume slider is taken out of the picture on top of that: the bridge hands over the raw level of each band and the pack divides the frame by its own recent peak, taken instantly on the way up and released over a couple of seconds on the way down. What the bars show is therefore the shape of the spectrum and the dynamics of the track rather than how loud someone likes their music, a quiet mix is lifted rather than lying flat, a loud one stops standing in the ceiling, and silence is answered by silence rather than by amplified noise. Band gains rise with frequency because music sheds roughly three decibels an octave, and without that tilt the bass bar simply stands at the ceiling. A peak meter was tried first and is kept as the fallback for a machine where the capture cannot open: it reports one number per interval, and on a mastered track that number barely moves, which is a visualiser standing still no matter what arithmetic is applied to it. The bars are not laid out by frequency - the bass sits at the end and the low mids in the middle - and each rises instantly and falls with inertia. Two dials in the customization window scale the whole thing without touching the bands themselves: one multiplies how far the sound drives the bars, the other how quickly they let go, both a quarter to double the default, and neither touches the rise, which stays instant because any smoothing on the way up is latency you can see. The row is painted from the cover rather than in the launcher accent, and it is the cover's own layout that is carried across: the artwork is reduced to a grid two rows deep and as many columns as there are bars, each sampled pixel splatted into the four cells around it, so a red top-left corner comes out as red at the top of the leftmost bar and neighbouring bars meet in a ramp instead of a step. The splatting weight is squared rather than plain bilinear, since a plain one spreads every pixel two cells in each direction - a third of the picture - and a subject on the cover smeared across the whole row instead of sitting where it is. Every bar is one gradient between its own two cells, anchored to the row rather than to the bar, so the colours stay where they are while the bars move rather than sliding up and down with the sound. Two stops and no more: the fill is a fan of triangles around the middle of the shape, so a stop partway down a side bleeds diagonally inwards, and on something as narrow as a bar a run of stops read as dark crosses laid over the row. Nothing is invented: no palette is generated and no hue is chosen, the pixels are simply averaged where they lie, which is why a black and white cover gives a silver ramp instead of falling back to the theme. A cell's colour and its lightness are taken from two different averages, because one weighting cannot serve both. The colour comes from a mean weighted by each pixel's own saturation, since a sixth of a picture usually holds a bright field and a dark subject and a flat mean of the two is mud rather than either; the lightness comes from the plain mean, because under the saturation weighting anything black counts for almost nothing and never reaches the row at all. The saturation lost to averaging - opposite hues cancel each other where a mean is taken - is given back afterwards, up to the mean saturation of the pixels that went into the cell and no further, so a mostly grey region cannot be dragged into a pure colour by a handful of vivid pixels. The whole row is then raised together by its own brightest cell rather than cell by cell, and the bottom of the range is compressed rather than clipped, so a dark cover stays legible against the glass without losing the difference between its shadows and its blacks. Each cell travels to its new track along an arc of hue rather than straight through grey, so the middle of the change is a colour and not a smear of it. Switching it off returns the bars to the accent. Under that row sit the same clock and a hairline of progress, while the name, frame rate and ping move into a second capsule that opens beside it - fixed at the resting height, so it never grows with the card - the way the real island splits when two things are live at once. There is no cross-fade between those states and no second avatar: two springs carry the whole layout, one from rest to playing and one from pill to card, and every size and position is read off them, so the picture grows and travels rather than being swapped, and only the one line that changes meaning fades from the name to the title in place. Playback is read from the Windows media session - the same channel every browser and player already reports to - so Chrome and its forks, Firefox and its forks, Edge, Opera, Brave, Yandex, Tor, Zen, Spotify, Yandex Music and the rest are covered without knowing anything about them in particular. A session carrying no metadata at all, or one whose properties can no longer be read, is passed over rather than chosen: that is what a reloaded tab leaves behind in the list, and taking it emptied the island while the music played on. A browser can also stop publishing the session outright - Firefox drops the media controller when the tab holding it is reloaded and does not raise it again until some new element starts, which on a music site means an advert or a quick run through several tracks - and that would fold the island away with the sound still coming out of the machine. So the bridge falls back to listening: it measures the output of the application that last held a session, or, when there was none, finds the loudest player among the machine's audio sessions, and keeps the island open as a single-row pill carrying the site's name where the title would be, the visualiser beside it, and no artwork, clock or progress, because none of those can be known. It says what it knows and nothing more; the moment Windows reports a session again the card comes back with it. The artwork comes from the same session: a small C# reader ships as source and is compiled on the machine against the metadata Windows already carries, since the scripting host alone cannot open the thumbnail stream, and the result is trimmed of the frame some players wrap around it, squared and masked before it becomes a texture. It lives in one file that each new track overwrites and the game deletes on the way out. A change of artwork is a turn rather than a swap. The card pivots about its own vertical axis, to the right when the track moved forward and to the left when it moved back, and because the interface is drawn in an orthographic projection - where a turn one way and a turn the other collapse to the same squeeze - the quad is cut into vertical strips and each is placed at its own depth, so the near half grows, the far half shrinks, and the side it is turning to can be read. Which side that is comes out of a stack of what has already been heard, since Windows never says which button was pressed: the previous track is the one on top of that stack and taking it pops it, anything else pushes the departing track and counts as forward, and a track that ran to its end is always forward, so a two-track playlist does not turn backwards every other time. Two texture slots are kept in rotation for this, because writing the arriving cover over the same address would close a texture still being drawn on the front face. The far side carries that cover mashed: the sample point is dragged through two layers of warped noise and mirrored back into the image rather than clamped to its edge, so the colours of the arriving cover fold into each other instead of streaking off the border, and a short spiral blur is laid over that. The far side is unwrapped in reverse, the way the back of a real card is printed, since past the right angle the strips run right to left and the arriving cover would otherwise be drawn mirrored for the whole second half and snap round at the end. The mash holds while the card opens and then clears on an eased fall that reaches nothing shortly before the turn does, so the card lands sharp instead of snapping out of a blur on the last frame, and the rounding of its corners is computed in the shader rather than read from the baked alpha, which the warp would have crawled outwards along with the colour. A pause folds the island back after three seconds, while a track that has stalled on the network - the position standing still while the session keeps reporting - is given ten, so a hiccup does not throw the player away. On anything that is not Windows the island simply never leaves its resting state.
*   **Hotbar**: replaces the vanilla one. The row sits on one glass tray; empty slots collapse away and the row re-centres itself, so only slots holding an item take up space. Every occupied slot carries the number of the key that selects it, so the mapping stays readable once the row has collapsed. A selected empty slot is the one case where that number has nothing to sit beside — the collapse leaves a lone blank cell with no way to tell which key it belongs to — so it fills with its number, set large and centred where the item would be. The selection is a raised glass lens that glides between slots and stretches along its travel before settling, the way a liquid highlight does; it pops on switch and carries the attack-strength meter. The bar slides out of view when the player stops interacting with it and slides back in on slot switch, pickup, item use or when a screen is opened (delay is configurable). The offhand cell appears only when the offhand is occupied. The name of the item that has just been selected rises above the tray on its own plate, set in the launcher's accent like the rest of the interface; a rarity that means something — uncommon, rare, epic — still overrides that with its own colour, while an ordinary item no longer arrives painted white by the game's own styling.
*   **Status**: hearts, armour, hunger, air, stamina and mount health are replaced with a slim stack in a screen corner of your choice — the currency readout mirrors it across the screen and lines up with it — health at the bottom with its value to the left, hunger (or mount health) above it, then armour, air and stamina, which fade in only when they are relevant. Stamina is read from whichever movement mod is present — the parkour mod's own gauge and the weapon mod's separate bar are both switched off, so two competing readouts become one row in the pack's own style. Damage leaves a ghost trail that drains behind the current value and low health pulses. Absorption is counted into the health value itself rather than being reported on a line of its own below the row: a player who has just eaten an enchanted golden apple reads `36/20`, so the number the eye goes to is the damage the player can still take. The experience bar and level are not drawn at all; the horse jump meter still appears above the hotbar while riding.
*   **Pulse**: the trace beside the health value is a real ECG waveform, and its rate answers to both wounds and effort. Losing health pushes it towards two hundred beats a minute the way it always did; draining stamina pushes it to a hundred and fifty on its own, so a player who has just sprinted across an island watches his own heart settle before it is calm again. Exhaustion climbs quickly and falls slowly, which is what makes it read as catching your breath rather than as a meter. Wounds and effort do not add up — the louder of the two sets the tempo, so a wounded player who is also winded keeps the rate his injuries call for. The screen effects of EnhancedVisuals are driven from that same waveform. The pack holds the mod's own beat timer permanently out of range, which makes it stop deciding when to fire, and raises its effects itself: the `heartbeatin` sample a fraction of a cycle ahead of the R spike, then the `heartbeatout` sample with the red wash and the blur exactly on it, all through the mod's own textures, durations and volume. One beat of the line is one beat on screen, at whatever rate the line is running. Injuries own that channel: while health is under the mod's threshold the wash and the blur come with every beat at the strength its own formula asks for. Exhaustion never touches the screen effects — a winded player gets the heartbeat sample at reduced volume and a vignette that closes in from the edges of the screen, deepening with fatigue and swelling on each beat before easing back. The mod's configuration file is never rewritten — only its runtime timer is touched — and either half can be switched off (`heartbeatSync`, `heartbeatFatigue`). If EnhancedVisuals is absent the bridge simply never binds. Death stops the heart rather than racing it: the waveform collapses into a flat line over a third of a second, the beat lift and the flicker that rides on it go with it, the screen effects stop being raised at all, and the whole HUD settles into a steady alert red with no pulse in it. Respawning restores every one of those on its own — the line fills back in, the tint drains, and any exhaustion carried into the grave is cleared on the way out.
*   **Effects**: potion icons become compact chips under the minimap with remaining duration, sliding in and out and blinking before expiry.
*   **Boss bars**: rendered through `CustomizeGuiOverlayEvent.BossEventProgress` in the same slim style (used by the combat timer).
*   **Objectives**: a permanent strip at the top of the screen carries the score — points held by your team, the match clock, points held by the enemy — with a pill per point underneath. Pills stay on screen for the whole match, carry a muted team dot and a progress hairline, and light up while a point is contested or has just changed hands; world markers are small dots with a distance label. When one team owns every regular point, the unlock of the final point is announced with a tinted banner and a bell instead of happening silently.
*   **Lobby**: the countdown block arrives and leaves the way every other element does instead of being switched on whole. The card lifts into place as it fades in, its width and height follow whatever it currently holds, so the switch from the countdown to the grace notice glides rather than cuts, the bar drains through the same smoothing the capture bars use, every new second gives the card a short lift and the last five pulse, and the list of players still missing rides in under it and leaves the same way.
*   **Minimap and map screen**: the minimap is deliberately the one surface that is not made of glass — a flat opaque panel, because a refracted, blurred capture under a map made the terrain under it unreadable. The live coordinates sit on a flat grey plate sized from the same measurement the type itself is set with. Your side's base is marked on both surfaces as a ring in the zone's own colour with its label above it, clamped to the rim of the minimap once it falls outside, so the way home is readable without opening anything. Hairline grid, directional player arrow, and themed buttons and sliders. The map is drawn from float-precision region quads with clamped sampling and follows the interpolated player position, so panning and zooming stay smooth and seam-free. Terrain stays live: the server repaints chunks the moment they are built, mined, flooded or blown up and pushes them to every player in the dimension, so a crater on the far side of the map appears on everyone's minimap within a fraction of a second. Whatever the client has already walked is redrawn into the region textures the moment it logs in, so a known world shows its map straight away instead of filling in again chunk by chunk. That cache is kept per world rather than per client, so two single-player worlds no longer overwrite each other's map. What the server hands out is scoped to the team: chunks a player streams up are stored under his team and pushed to his teammates only, live repaints go to the teams that already know that ground, and a player on no team neither sends nor receives - he walks his own map. So a crater an enemy made shows up for his side the moment one of them loads it, and stays unknown to everyone else until they look at it themselves. Ground nobody has walked can be brought in on demand: an operator names a radius in blocks and the server reads every chunk around him, generating the ones that have never been visited, paints them and streams them back to him and to his team. The walk starts at the centre and spirals out, so the ground nearest the player fills in first, and it is metered by time rather than by a chunk count, because a chunk that has to be generated costs tens of milliseconds in the server thread. Chunks that arrive are drawn into the region textures as one batch and handed to the GPU once, instead of one upload of a megabyte texture per chunk the way a full map sync used to do it.
*   **Notifications**: every message the pack's modules send is intercepted client-side and shown as a stacked toast in the top-left corner instead of the chat. Each toast drains a bar showing how long it will stay, messages that carry a remaining time (capture cooldown, return-to-zone, surrender vote, quarry cooldown, airdrop timers, immortality) count down live in place instead of stacking a new toast every second, and such a toast now leaves after the time any message gets rather than sitting on screen for the whole countdown while its bar keeps telling the truth; while one of the pack's own screens is open the answer is written on a plate under that screen in the launcher accent instead of arriving behind it as a toast, and airdrop announcements linger ten seconds longer. Toasts about a point being taken from the defending team are tinted with the alert accent, pulse, and are refreshed by the server every second, so the countdown stays honest and the toast disappears shortly after the attackers are pushed off. Anything the HUD already carries never becomes a toast at all: the lobby countdown and the players it is waiting for, the surrender vote, the start and the end of a capture, and every payout, because the currency card flashes the amount it gained by itself.
*   **Chat**: the vanilla chat keeps its own layout and its own input handling; only its motion is taken over. The input line rises out of the bottom edge when the chat is opened, and it is drawn while the keyboard is already live, so the first keystroke lands whether or not the line has finished arriving. An incoming message pushes the stack up: the new rows are dropped below the last one and carried back into place, clipped to the bottom of the chat area so they slide out from under it rather than appearing in the empty band over the hotbar. Lines that arrive in the same tick travel as one, and a burst is capped, so a flood of messages never turns into a jump. History is handled apart from that, and per row rather than in bulk: opening the chat slides in from the left edge every line that had already faded out, in a fraction of a second, while the rows that were on screen a moment ago stay exactly where the eye left them, so a chat that is still talking is never jolted and a chat that has gone quiet is dealt out of the left edge in full. A message that arrives after that silence, with the chat closed, carries the whole block in the same way. History scrolled back by hand is left alone entirely. All three offsets are snapped to whole physical pixels, so type stays as sharp mid-animation as it is at rest. `chatMotion` turns the layer off.
*   **Typography**: the interface is set in Inter, shipped in three weights, and the wordmark in the title screen is set in a display cut of its own — Archivo instanced at `wght=900 wdth=125`, an expanded black grotesque, welded to Inter Bold for Cyrillic and everything else Archivo does not carry, so the one file behind the title face covers the whole character set at a single em. The greeting on first launch carries a third cut of its own, Inter instanced at `opsz=32 wght=300` — a light display face, the only thin type in the pack — built by `tools/build-hero-font.py`. All of it is under the SIL Open Font License; `tools/build-title-font.py` rebuilds the wordmark file from a downloaded variable font and the shipped bold cut, and `licenses/` carries the notices. Glyphs come from signed distance field atlases baked from the three cuts, so a letter is reconstructed from its outline at whatever size the screen asks for instead of being scaled up from a fixed bake. The same outlines are offered in five renderings, and the game asks which one to use on first launch, right after the visual mode. **Crisp** reconstructs the edge from the distance field in one sample, which holds a thin hard edge and is what the pack shipped until now. **Smooth** widens that edge over about three pixels and lifts the gamma to keep the weight, which is what a large monitor with a coarse pixel wants, since a one-pixel edge on a physically large pixel reads as a staircase. **Screen** abandons the field entirely: glyphs are rasterised through the platform outline engine at six times the size they are drawn, packed into an atlas as they are first asked for, and resolved on the fly by nine taps spread across the pixel footprint, so a pixel gets the share of the outline that actually falls inside it rather than a distance to the nearest edge. That is the same principle desktop text rendering uses, and it is the only one that reads as area coverage rather than an analytic edge. **Sharp**, which the pack now starts on, is raster too but does not spread the coverage: the taps are averaged over the pixel footprint, because a single tap under minification tears the stroke apart, and the result is cut by a threshold about a pixel wide, so the stroke comes out thinner than the area rendering and its edge lands on one pixel instead of fading over three. **Velvet** is the area rendering again, thickened where it counts: the stroke is strengthened once at bake time by blending each texel toward the peak of its eight neighbours, which fattens the stem without closing the counters, and the shader lifts small text more gently than **Screen** does. It costs one pass over a glyph for the life of the game rather than extra taps per pixel. Stroke weight is a knob rather than a property of the rendering: one value shifts the threshold in the raster renderings and the distance in the field ones, so the same three settings — thin, regular, heavy — read the same whichever of the four is chosen, and the wizard asks for it on its own page with the halo strength on a slider beneath the samples. All five carry identical advances and ink centres, measured to the pixel on screen, so nothing in the layout moves when the choice changes: the row sits in Customization under Interface, with the weight and the halo on sliders directly beneath it, and switches without a restart. Only the chosen rendering is resident; the others are built solely while the sample cards are on screen and dropped when they leave. Regular carries body copy, semibold carries names, values and item titles, and bold carries every label: widget captions, screen titles, zone markers in the world and the plates on the map. The TrueType path stays in the jar and takes over on its own if an atlas fails to load; what follows describes how that fallback keeps its edge. The pack ships its own typeface with a matching bold cut, baked at several resolutions. Text size is snapped so the baked glyph lands on the screen one texel per pixel, and at that point the atlas is sampled without any interpolation — a glyph is never stretched, never blended, so labels are as sharp as the game's own font at every GUI scale, and small text stays legible instead of turning into a grey smear. Letter spacing is exact: the game's TrueType loader counts a glyph's left side bearing twice, which pushes letters like `l`, `n` and `D` a couple of pixels off their pen position and leaves the gaps around them visibly uneven, so the shipped typeface carries no side bearings in its horizontal metrics and the ink lands where the design puts it. On top of that the pack sets type itself, one glyph at a time, advancing the pen in whole physical pixels — every letter sits on the pixel grid and the rhythm of a word is the same everywhere it appears. Widget labels use the real bold cut, and all text a screen or the HUD draws for itself — titles, list rows, tooltips, chat, item counts, damage numbers — is rendered in the same typeface rather than the vanilla one.
*   **Kill feed**: kills reported by TACZ and Superb Warfare are taken over from their own overlays and shown as ordinary toasts, so they stack with everything else instead of drawing on top of it. Headshots read differently from plain kills. The streak counter TACZ draws by the crosshair is replaced by one toast of the pack's own: the pack counts the kills itself over a window of fifteen seconds, every kill inside the window raises the number in place and starts the window again, and the toast holds for those fifteen seconds with its bar draining to the end of the window.
*   **Item facts**: tooltips carry what the pack knows about an item — the full weapon table for TACZ guns, nutrition and saturation for food, remaining durability for anything that wears out. Attributes contributed by ParCool and TACZ read as plain names, and bullet resistance from TACZ and Superb Warfare is mirrored between the two attributes, so a single piece of armour protects against both.
*   **Vehicles**: while a player rides a Superb Warfare vehicle the right-hand stack (ammo, microphone, currency) and the status card both lift above that mod's own vehicle panel instead of hiding underneath it. Only the docked instrument panel is measured, and only where it runs under our own column: the ammunition name, the gunner's reticle and the hit markers ride the aim point rather than the bottom of the screen, so counting them lifted the card halfway up the screen the moment the camera went to third person. What counts as the panel is assembled from the bottom up — the deepest thing the vehicle HUD draws in that column, then whatever touches it — so a widget floating clear above it is left out, and anything drawn fully transparent is skipped outright, since the panel reserves room with invisible placeholders it never fills.
*   **Main menu**: the panorama is replaced with a slow drifting light field on near-black, the wordmark, and one column of buttons in the pack's style. The wordmark is set in the display cut at wide tracking with no spaces of its own in the string, and it is underlined by a short accent bar measured off its own width rather than a fixed one, so the bar never outruns the letters on a narrow window. Under it the build line is a lockup rather than a caption: the words sit small and widely tracked between two hairlines that start a fixed gap from the measured ends of the text, so the three pieces stay centred on each other whatever the language sets. The field is evaluated per pixel rather than assembled from rings of geometry: layered noise is folded through itself twice, so the light gathers into drifting smoke and thin filaments that never repeat, and three elliptical cores wander through it on their own paths while the noise pushes them off course. Nothing in it has a visible edge: the falloff is a real gaussian and the shader dithers its own gradient, which is what keeps the dark field from breaking into bands. No overlay grain is laid on top of the screen - the ordered pattern it drew read as a dot grid on large displays. The pointer carries a soft halo that follows it with a lag. The build version sits in the bottom-right corner, set in the faint accent, and it is not a dead caption: every few seconds a crest walks along it, brightening and lifting each character as it passes while a short accent dash travels under the line at the same place, lit by the same off-screen halo the wordmark uses. The dash is placed by the pen rather than by a measured width, so it tracks the letters exactly, and the whole pass is skipped entirely between runs, which is what keeps a full-screen bloom out of the idle frame. On entry the interface condenses out of light. The screen is drawn once into an off-screen surface, and one full-screen pass rebuilds it. Nothing in the pass is built from a grid or from cells: a scalar field is folded out of layered noise warped through itself twice, laid over a slow diagonal ramp and a few drifting seeds, and a threshold walks across that field for the length of the animation. Where the threshold has not yet arrived there is nothing at all; where it is passing, the material is pulled in from a short offset along the same warp that shapes the field, held out of focus, and lit in the launcher accent, and it stays faint for exactly as long as it is still being carried, so nothing solid is ever drawn out of place; where it has passed, the offset and the blur are gone and the colour has settled back to its own. Because the field is noise rather than geometry the arriving front has no edge anywhere - it is a wandering tongue of light that crosses the wordmark, then the rule, then each row of buttons in turn, and the interface is simply there behind it. A thread of accent light rides the front itself, brightest at the middle of each pixel's own transition and gone by the end of it, and high-frequency noise scatters glints along it. Offsets, blur radius and drift are measured in interface units and multiplied by the GUI scale, so the effect reads the same at every resolution, and the sweep is calibrated to the measured range of the field so that the first material appears in the opening moments and the last lands on the final frame. At the end the pass resolves to the untouched capture and finishes identical, pixel for pixel, to an ordinary frame. A click or a key press cuts it short, and `menuIntro` turns it off. The same pass carries every screen the pack draws for itself, from the `/bc` panels to the shop, the customization window and the zone and mark managers, since all of them descend from one glass screen: they arrive the same way the title does, in under a second, and they leave by burning. On the way out the threshold runs backwards over a field whose ramp is radial rather than diagonal, so the hole opens at the centre and eats outward. Nothing is carried, tinted or darkened on the way out: the rim of the hole neither smears the rows nor lays a dark band ahead of itself, because either one reads as the interface fading rather than being eaten. What is left fragments into islands on its own, which is the threshold doing it and not any geometry. In a world the burn runs for a second and a half so it can be watched; on the title screen it keeps its old half second. Opening another of the pack's screens no longer cuts a burn short: up to three of them burn at once, each on its own clock, and the new screen materialises over them as it always did. A screen on its way out is redrawn every frame rather than frozen, so its glass keeps answering the camera while it goes. Under sharpness and splinter that is carried down to the piece: what shows through a shard is read from where that shard hangs now, not from where it broke off, so one that has drifted a third of a panel away shows the world behind its new place instead of the view it left with.
*   **Panels in the world**: a screen the pack draws for itself is bound to the camera the whole time it is open, so it sits on the frame exactly as a flat panel does and nothing about it moves when the world keeps running behind it. It detaches on the way out. The moment the screen closes, the panel is pinned to the spot in the world it was covering, two blocks out and square to the view it had, at a size that puts it on the same pixels it already occupied; the camera is free again, and the panel burns away in place and leans with the view instead of hanging on as a photograph of the last frame. What burns is a live frame, not a capture: the closed screen keeps drawing itself for as long as the burn lasts, flat and in its own environment, and only the finished composite is carried onto that plane. The two were independent all along - nothing about the content has to pass through the perspective for the panel to hang in the world. The environment a screen renders in - the substituted font, the muted vanilla widgets, the restyled scrollbars - is a single begin and end pair rather than a pile of event handlers, so a closed screen wears exactly what an open one wears, and the off-screen surface the pass draws into carries a depth buffer of its own, so a three-dimensional item keeps its faces in order there the way it does on the frame. The glass in the burning panel refracts the world as it is now, and it refracts it from where the panel hangs rather than from where it was closed: the sample is carried across by the plane's own homography, so walking away pushes fresh world through the glass instead of leaving the player's hand frozen inside it. The carry covers the whole scene the pass draws into, not just the panel's own sample, so an element with nothing of ours beneath it - the customization preview, the shop's search field, the buttons in its header - reads the world from where it now hangs rather than from where the screen was closed. Outside the burn that carry is the identity and costs nothing. Several panels can hang at once: closing a screen while an earlier one is still burning leaves that one where it was left instead of wiping it off the frame, and the only burn that gives way is a flat one, which has no place in the world and would sit exactly where the next screen opens. They stack by distance rather than by age: a panel hangs in a perspective of its own, so its depth compares with nothing else on the frame, and none of them writes depth at all - the nearest is simply drawn last, instead of an older one cutting its own rectangle out of a newer. The refraction capture is set aside for the length of each burn as well. It is taken once a frame and shared by everything drawn in that frame, and a closed screen redrawing itself replaces it with a capture of its own surface, which left a screen opened over a burning one refracting the burning panel instead of the world - a menu that arrived visibly darker than it settles. The whole thing is client-side; no packet carries it and nobody else sees it. The pass samples the capture by the coordinate it was taken at rather than by the pixel it lands on, so the pattern stays pinned to the panel, and its mask can never reach a pixel the interface did not cover. `worldPanel` in the config turns it off and burns the panel on the frame instead.
*   **Screens**: every vanilla screen, settings included, is carried over to the same style, and every one of them now opens the way the pack's own screens do — the world dims smoothly instead of snapping to a grey wash, and the content settles in from a slight scale. The title of a screen is no longer set on bare sky: it rides the same glass pill the toasts use, cut to the width of its own text, so it stays readable over a bright horizon. The dim and the capture are taken over at the screen's own background call, so screens the pack never replaced — the telemetry page, the world-saving screen, a mod's configuration page — stop falling back to the dirt texture. Buttons, sliders and scrollbars are drawn as the pack's own glass widgets — the original widget is hidden but kept alive, so input, focus and tooltips behave exactly as the game intends — and a single label size is used across the whole interface, so no two controls anywhere disagree about type size; a label too long for its button is clipped to the button and drifts, the way the game does it, rather than being set smaller than its neighbours. The dirt background and the list header and footer bars are replaced by the same drifting light field the main menu uses, running off the same clock, so walking from the menu into the options changes nothing but the content. Checkboxes and text fields are drawn in the same material, tooltips everywhere are recoloured to the pack's palette, and a widget that sits inside a scrolling list is clipped to that list rather than to a fixed band around its rows, which is what used to leave the far column of a mod's configuration page blank; the voice chat's own window plate is replaced by the pack's glass; and the treatment is extended to the configuration screens of the mods that ship with the pack — cloth-config, Embeddium, Distant Horizons, the voice chat, the movement mod and the rest — so walking into a mod's settings does not walk out of the interface. Screens that hold items (inventories, chests, workbenches) keep their own artwork. A widget lights under the pointer and, when the keyboard is driving, under the focus — never after a click that moved the focus onto it and left the pointer elsewhere, which used to leave reset and toggle rows lit long after they were pressed. Text fields are drawn by the pack rather than by the game: the line is set in the middle of the field instead of along its top edge, which is where the game puts it whenever the border is off, and the caret is a rounded bar in the accent that slides to its new place and stretches along the way rather than a blinking underscore out of the font. It stands after the insertion point rather than centred on it — centred, half its width lies on the letter before it and eats the stem of a thin one — and it carries no glow of its own, since a glow painted over the line eats the same letters and one painted under it chews the alpha of the off-screen surface a closing screen burns into. It holds solid for half a second after a keystroke before it starts breathing again, the selection is a soft accent slab behind the letters, and the field itself answers focus with an inner light and a hairline of accent around it. Whatever the field's own formatter paints — command syntax in the console block, for one — is kept. The measuring pen is shared with the game's own arithmetic for the click and the horizontal scroll, so the caret lands on the letter that was clicked however long the line is, and the plate around a field no longer reaches high enough to touch the caption above it. The tab strips the newer screens use — the one over world creation among them — are carried over as well: the black band, both seams and the dirt-textured tabs are gone, and a tab is a glass button with an accent rule under the selected one.
*   **Sound**: the interface has its own voice, and there are three of them. **Note blocks** is the original, mixed from tuned vanilla samples with nothing of its own in the jar. **Future** and **Pop** are sampled sets shipped with the pack — one synthetic and airy, one short and dry — with their own answers for hover, press, either direction of a toggle, confirm, refusal, a notification, grabbing and letting go of a slider. A cue is looked up in the chosen set and falls back to the note-block voice when the set has nothing for it, so the three are interchangeable rather than parallel. The set is asked for on first launch, where pointing at a card plays it and silences the button's own hover so only the candidate is heard, and it sits in Customization under Interface. Whichever is chosen, it clicks rather than plays: one dry tick per slot switch, separate answers for hover and press, a click per slider notch, screens opening and closing, effect chips arriving and leaving. Buttons and sliders suppress the vanilla widget click so only the pack's own sample is heard, and the hover cue has its own switch for players who would rather not have one. Only the signal that must cut through is tonal — a low bell for an alert toast; low health says nothing, since the heartbeat already speaks for it. Every cue is rate-limited, and `uiSoundVolume` scales or silences the whole layer. ExtraSounds is left in place and keeps every action the pack has no answer for - item clicks in a container, typing, chat, drops - but where both speak for the same thing the pack is the one heard: a container opening or closing, a hotbar slot switching, and an effect arriving or leaving are cut off on its side before it reaches the sound engine. The hook is applied only when that mod is actually installed, so nothing changes for an instance without it, and it steps aside altogether when the interface layer is silenced.
*   **Pause menu**: the mod list button is gone from both the title screen and the pause grid; the quit button moves up into the row it left behind and the row at the bottom becomes the match action — team select while the lobby is open, surrender once the player is on a side — so the two full-width rows stay symmetrical instead of leaving a hole where a button used to be. Advancements are greyed out while connected to a server. The dim a screen lays over the world covers the HUD with it, and it keeps covering the HUD while it fades back out after the screen closes, so the interface no longer snaps back to full brightness while the world behind it is still dark.
*   **Voice**: the microphone badge above the ammo card reads the voice chat's own state, and it distinguishes a muted microphone from a missing one — with no capture device present, or the mic thread refusing to start, the badge is joined by a plate saying so. Mute is only reported while voice activation is the active mode: in push-to-talk the mod ignores the mute flag, so showing it there left the badge crossed out while the player was audible to everyone. Group screens are refused outright.
*   **Atmosphere**: the light field behind the menu is not a static backdrop but the pack's depth of field. Three values — a small push in, a vignette, and a softening of the field — are held on their own easings and handed straight to the field shader: the push is a division of the sample point, so it costs nothing and stays sharp, and the softening drops octaves out of the noise and renormalises what is left rather than blurring the frame, which is what keeps a procedural field from turning into porridge. It is applied to the cloud layer alone: the two noise fields that warp and swirl the sampling point keep their full detail, because softening those moves the point every cloud is read from, and the whole field slides across the screen as the mood changes — which reads as the animation being put on fast-forward. Each screen names the mood it wants: the title screen asks for none of it, the first-run wizard asks for all of it and takes about two seconds to get there, and anything opened over the menu — options, the server list, the world list, the customization window — asks for a lighter version that arrives in a quarter of a second, so the effect is already in place by the time the window is. What is being removed sets the pace of its own removal, so the wizard dissolves as unhurriedly as it gathered while a window comes back in about half a second — a little slower than it left, because the field brightening under a window that is still on screen reads as a flash rather than a release. The traversal is a timed one eased at both ends rather than an exponential chase: an exponential spends more than half its change in the first fifth of the time, which is exactly the shape that reads as a snap no matter how long the tail is.
*   **Glow**: large type is lit rather than outlined. The letters are drawn once onto a black canvas the size of the frame, run through the same blur pyramid the glass uses, and added back under the text, which is the difference between a soft halo and the eight offset copies of a glyph that a stepped outline is made of. The canvas is opaque black on purpose: blurring straight alpha drags emptiness into the halo and kills it. The strength of the composite is set through the shader colour rather than by writing the uniform directly, since the engine overwrites that uniform from the shader colour immediately before the draw — written the other way the light is silently always at full, which is what a glow that switches on rather than rises looks like. The pass returns the frame to whichever buffer was bound before it, so it works inside the off-screen the screens arrive through as well as on the main target, and it puts back the shader colour it found rather than resetting it: a layer that is fading out fades its own light with it, and everything drawn after the halo keeps the transparency of the layer it belongs to.
*   **First launch**: the title screen is not the first thing a new install shows. A greeting arrives instead, over the same drifting field and nothing else, and it is built the way a landing page builds a hero. It waits for the loading overlay to be gone first: Mojang's overlay draws the screen underneath itself through the last second of its fade, so a cascade started on the screen's first frame would half play out behind a red plate. Only the field is drawn while the plate is there — the title screen holds its own intro back the same way — and the greeting begins once the plate has. The wordmark comes in first, small and widely tracked in the display cut; an accent hairline opens under it from the middle outwards, by width rather than by opacity; the greeting itself lands third, set large in a light display cut of Inter at negative tracking, which is the one place in the pack where type is thin rather than bold; the line under it and the invitation to press anything follow, and the invitation breathes and floats on its own once it has landed. Everything on this screen is drawn off the pixel grid: the type is normally snapped to whole device pixels to stay crisp, which turns a slow drift of two units into eight visible steps. Each line climbs a little as it fades in and its tracking closes from a wider setting, so the type assembles rather than appears; a key or a click before all of it has landed lands it at once rather than being ignored. Once the type has settled it does not sit still: light walks along the greeting letter by letter, each letter lifting slightly and brightening as the crest reaches it, and when the crest has left the line a hairline draws itself under the first word of the line below, left to right, then retreats from the right as the next word takes its turn — and the last word keeps its rule and lights it in the accent before both fade out together, since a run that simply stopped read as a dropped frame. The rules are measured by walking the same pen the text is drawn with, glyph by glyph and rounding the same way, because a word measured on its own is not the width it occupies inside a tracked line, and the difference is enough to leave a rule visibly short of its word. The light itself does not switch on with the type: once the greeting has landed the halo eases up from nothing over about a second and a half. The wordmark above and the invitation below hold a steady breathing glow through all of it. Nothing of this begins before the field has finished gathering behind it. Answering it does not cut — the lines spread their tracking back out and leave upward while the first question rises from below, and every question after that changes the same way, one travel of a little over half a second with the widgets rebuilt at the midpoint, so nothing ever pops. Input is refused for the length of a turn, which is what keeps a double click from answering the next question. Nine questions follow, each a single choice with the reasoning written under it: the visual mode, the density of the glass, whether the glass takes its colour from the launcher theme, what the text is set in, the font rendering, how heavy that text is drawn, the interface sound set, the voice chat and the Discord status. The sound question carries the volume with it rather than asking twice: a slider sits under the three cards, and it needs no sample button, since it clicks at every notch in the interface's own voice. Any answer can be taken back - a step returns to the previous question, on a button beside the dots or on Escape, and the page slides back the way it came rather than forward. Three of them show what they are asking about rather than describing it — the text colour page sets the same three lines in each answer's own inks, so what is on trial is not the colour of the heading but whether the dimmed and the faint ink stay apart from it once the accent takes over, the font page draws its three samples in the actual renderings and drops the two that lose, the sound page carries a click and a hover probe inside each card, which play that set and nothing else — they answer no question and are silent in their own voice, so a player can hear a scheme before committing to it — and the glass colour question carries a swatch of each answer inside its button, drawn by the button's own skin so that it rises with it under the pointer and shrinks with it under a press rather than standing still while the button moves. The weight page is built the same way: its three cards are set in the weight each of them offers, and the halo slider under them moves the real knob, so the samples light up under the pointer as it travels. The voice page is not a card page at all but four rows that write straight into Simple Voice Chat — speakers, microphone, whether the microphone opens on its own or on a key, and which key — and it ends by marking that mod's own onboarding as done, so its screens never appear over the pack's. The key row waits for a key or a mouse button and takes Escape as a cancel rather than as a step back, and it goes dim while voice activation is chosen instead of leaving the page. The same four rows live in Customization under Interface, so a device can be changed later without going through another mod's screens. The glass density question is only asked of a player who chose liquid glass, since the other two modes have no body to dim; the voice question is only asked of a player who has the mod; skipped or answered, every step is written to `battlecraft/setup.json` by name, so a question added later is the only one a returning player is asked. Dots along the bottom of the panel count the questions and mark which one is open. The last answer is not a cut either: the running dot fades where it stands on the final question and a crest walks the whole row instead, lighting each dot in the accent as it passes, and the panel only starts burning once the crest has left the last one. Nothing in that is tied to a step number — the crest is measured off the number of dots, so a question added to the enum joins the finale on its own.
*   **Discord**: the pack reports to Discord over the local IPC pipe — the phase of the match, the team the player is on, whether a vote is running, and a button through to the repository — refreshed on every phase change and every half minute otherwise. It is a switch rather than a fixture: it is asked about on first launch, sits in Customization under Interface, and turning it off shuts the connection down instead of leaving a stale card up. The handshake that reads the player's Discord picture for the island is a separate thing on a separate thread, so the avatar survives the status being turned off.
*   **Customization**: a palette button sits in the bottom-left corner of both the title screen and the pause menu, and it opens the whole interface for editing. In the menu the screen keeps the drifting light field behind it; in game it dims the world the way the pause menu does. A live preview panel stands beside the controls and is drawn with the very calls the HUD uses, so a status card with a running ECG, a button, a menu row, a bar, a toast and the three levels of body text all answer to a slider the moment it moves. The glass tab opens with a switch that hands the glass colour to the launcher theme and with the colour of the glass itself, so the material is set where it is seen instead of from the colour list; the theme is mixed into the factory grey by a fixed share rather than replacing it, which is what keeps the top-to-bottom fall in density that makes a panel read as a body, and picking a glass colour by hand takes the switch off the same way picking an accent takes the launcher off the accent; one colour drives the whole body gradient and the darker bottom is derived from it, so a panel keeps its depth whatever hue it is given. The rest of the tab exposes the material itself rather than a quality switch: body density and panel opacity, edge softness, corner shape and both radii, the width and strength of the lens along the rim, its falloff, the centre zoom, how many rings it is cut into and how many of them fall on the edge, the colour dispersion, the depth of the blur pyramid with the kernel width of each pass, the travelling sheen and its period, and the spread, density and white mix of the halo around every letter. Ten presets set all of it at once — liquid glass as designed, clear glass with the blur switched off entirely, a thick lens with no tint, crystal for maximum refraction, frosted for a dense body, blur for clear panels over a blurred capture, plain for a machine that should pay for a gradient and nothing else, and the rest between them. The chosen preset is remembered and named at the top of the tab: resetting the glass returns every slider to that preset's own values rather than to the factory ones, and resetting everything at once puts liquid glass back. A preset also picks the path it is drawn through — the shader pane or the older ring path — and the sliders the inactive path cannot read are greyed out rather than zeroed, so a value set for one path survives a trip through the other. The colours tab covers the accent, all three levels of text, the alert colour, the menu background, panels, the trough, the stroke, the four zone colours and the default map mark; each opens a picker with an HSV field, a hue strip, an alpha strip that reaches the colour it is set on rather than being discarded, a hex field, a row of swatches, a pair of buttons that copy the value to the clipboard and paste one back from it, and a button that drops that one colour back to the theme. The picker is a window rather than a modal plate: it is dragged anywhere on the screen, several stand open at once, each carries a close cross and answers Esc, and the screen underneath stays live - rows still light up and take clicks everywhere a window does not cover, and nothing reaches them where one does. Clicking the row that opened a window closes it again. Windows arrive and leave through the same pass the screens use, and through whichever of the two motion sets is chosen: under light and burn they resolve out of a front that radiates from the window's own centre rather than the middle of the screen and burn away the same way, and under sharpness and splinter they focus in out of a blur and break into Voronoi shards that drift apart, lose focus as they go, fade one after another and hold a narrow accent-lit rim along every edge. The bottom of the tab carries a second button that clears every colour at once, so a palette someone has played with can be put back without hunting through the list. Two switches sit above the swatch rows rather than beside them: one paints every piece of interface text in the accent - the main, dimmed and faint inks all take its hue, each keeping the lightness step it has in the factory palette so the hierarchy survives the recolour - and the other does the same for the HUD alone - every element the pack draws over the world, the island among them - which is how it is drawn out of the box, and it leaves the menus and screens on the plain ink. A colour picked by hand for the text wins over the first of the two; the HUD switch wins over that hand-picked colour wherever the HUD is drawn, and a slot tint set in the HUD editor wins over all of it. The two switches are exclusive: the first already paints the HUD along with everything else, so turning it on drops the second and greys its row out with the reason written on it, and the row comes back the moment the first is off. Both travel to their new inks the same way every other tone does, since the HUD holds a wash of its own rather than swapping one trio for the other between two frames. Touching the accent stops the interface from following the launcher theme, and one button hands that back. A colour never lands in one frame. Every tone the interface draws from - the accent, its two quieter fills, both trios of text and the trio the HUD reads - travels to its new value over a little under half a second, and it travels around the hue circle by the shorter way rather than straight across the channels, because a straight line runs through grey and the interface washes out in the middle of the change instead of changing hue. Saturation is lifted a third of the way over the middle of that arc and set back down at the end, so the change reads as a colour sweeping rather than a colour dissolving. A step small enough to be a drag inside the picker is taken at once, so the whole interface still answers the cursor without a lag behind it; only a real jump - a swatch, a preset, a whole theme, a reset - is worth travelling. The same applies to the swatch in the colour list, and to the tint set on a single HUD element, which fades in and out of the element's own colour rather than replacing it. Nothing is written to disk mid-drag: the file is flushed a tick after the last change, and everything lives in `battlecraft/customization.bcfg` next to the instance. A fifth tab keeps the player's own presets: a name, four ticks for what the snapshot carries - glass, colours, interface, HUD layout - and a list on the left that applies, overwrites or deletes what is already saved. Each preset is one `.bcfg` file in `battlecraft/presets`, so it can be sent to another player and dropped back into the folder the button opens. Applying one lays out the sections it carries whole and leaves the rest of the settings alone, and a knob the file never mentioned falls back to its factory value instead of keeping whatever stood there before.
*   **Heartbeat flicker**: the status card lifts, whitens and tints itself on every beat of the ECG, which reads as the whole card flickering while the trace runs. It is off by default and switched on from the interface tab; with it off the beat still draws the trace, it just stops modulating the card, the reading and the alert tint, each of which holds its steady value instead.
*   **Interface editor**: there is no editor screen any more - the layout is edited in the running game. Opening the chat lifts every HUD element into an outline of its own, one after another rather than all at once, and the world keeps running underneath with nothing dimmed over it, so an element is judged against the frame it actually lives on. Nineteen of them are addressable - hotbar, item name, status card, ammo, microphone, currency, vision goggles, minimap, effects, toasts, the objective strip, the capture bar, knockdown, zone prompts, titles, the lobby countdown, the lobby warning plate, the island and the surrender vote panel - and one that is not on screen at that moment still gets a frame where it would sit, named and marked as hidden, so a layout can be arranged whether or not the match happens to be showing it. The grid itself is invisible until it is used: a dragged element that comes within reach of the safe frame, of either third of the screen, or of an edge or centre line of another element pulls that one line out of nothing in the accent colour and lets go of it again when the element moves on. Brought close to another element instead, it stops at a fixed distance - the same distance everywhere, for every shape - and a green seam appears along the touching side to say the two are now bound: the docked element holds that gap from then on, follows its neighbour when it grows, moves aside when it appears, and returns to its own place when the neighbour leaves the screen. Everything else is kept apart by a solver that runs every frame: a panel that has just appeared or just changed size holds its ground and the settled ones give way along whichever axis they are least clear of, each carried by a spring rather than snapped, so a widening ammo counter or an arriving toast pushes its neighbours out and lets them drift back on their own, and nothing is pushed past the safe frame - an element with no room left simply stays where it is. The selected element carries a handle at its top-left and bottom-right corner in the accent colour; dragging one resizes the element around the opposite corner and only ever uniformly, so nothing is stretched out of proportion. Three elements are docked from the start, because their place was never their own: the item name and the action bar hold above the hotbar, the effect chips under the minimap, and each of them falls back to its own corner when what it leans on is not on screen - which is what keeps their frames where the elements will actually appear rather than in the corner the anchor alone would put them. A dock of that kind is the element's own place rather than a decision of the player's, so the corrections that keep a stack in order still apply to it: the action bar stands above the room kept for the item name instead of sharing the hotbar's edge with it, and the solver refuses to push a docked element onto the thing it leans on, which is what used to seat a message card over the hotbar cells whenever both were on screen at once. A right click on empty space offers one line, resetting every position at once, and a right click on an element opens a small panel at the pointer - the element's name, a switch that turns that block of the interface off and on, opacity, a row of colour swatches with the theme default among them, a line that undocks it, one that centres it and one that resets it - and it grows out of the point it was opened from and closes on Esc. Its rows arrive in a cascade rather than together, each sliding the last few units into place a beat after the one above it, and they leave the same way in reverse. A row under the pointer warms rather than switching: the highlight fills in, the label brightens and steps a unit to the right. The switches slide - the knob travels its track and the fill grows behind it - and they are drawn with the switch corner shape from the customization window rather than the panel one, so they match the switches in every other screen instead of following the general corner setting. The swatch row carries one ring rather than an outline that jumps between colours: picking a swatch sends the ring travelling to it and a ripple opens out of it and settles, so the choice reads as one selection moving instead of two appearing. The panel is tinted from the text colour rather than the accent and sits well below it in brightness, so whatever palette is set, the writing on it stays legible. An element under the pointer answers before it is clicked: the outline swells slightly, gathers weight and lights a faint fill, and its name rises into place instead of appearing. One that is not on screen at that moment draws itself as a sample instead - a card the size of its frame with its name set in it - so it can be sized and placed against something that looks like the real thing; the minimap is the one exception and only ever lights its outline, since a map that renders on demand is a map that can be read when it should not be. Nothing is saved mid-drag: the file is flushed a tick after the last change, and closing the chat commits whatever was being moved.
*   **World and server lists**: the vanilla selection screens are replaced by a pair of the pack's own. A list column of glass cards sits beside a preview column that fills in as soon as a card is picked, a search box above the list filters by name or address as you type, and the actions live in the preview rather than in a row at the bottom. Cards slide in one after another, a title too long for its card scrolls itself, and a second click on a card is the same as pressing Play. Ping is drawn as five procedural bars instead of the vanilla sprite, and the preview column lays itself out from the height it is given, so it never runs under the buttons on a short window. Everything the vanilla screens ask before opening a world — the symlink warning, the backup prompt, the version confirmation — is asked in the same order.
*   **Loading screens**: connecting to a server, loading a world and reading level data all draw the same frame instead of the vanilla dirt background. A pixel mark assembles itself out of nothing along a jittered diagonal, each pixel dropping in from a few cells above, and once it is whole it breathes by a single cell and a band of light sweeps across it and waits off-screen before coming back. Directly under it runs a thin accent bar: a real fraction where the game reports one, and a smoothed pendulum where it does not, because a bar that runs evenly in a circle reads as a hang. World loading adds a mosaic of the spawn chunks below the caption, one square per chunk, coloured by how far that chunk has come.
*   **Boot screen**: the same frame covers the launch of the game itself. The loader's own overlay keeps running — it is what waits for the reload, calls back and takes itself off — but it is clipped to nothing while it does, so the pack's frame is the only thing on screen and the loader's screen never shows through it. Once the reload is done the frame fades on the vanilla schedule and the main menu ignites out of the dark behind it. The halo around the progress bar is drawn by hand there rather than through the blur pyramid, because the core shaders arrive with the end of the very reload this frame is watching: one path from the first frame to the last instead of an unlit bar that suddenly lights near the end. The window that FML owns before mods are loaded is still the loader's own.
*   **Lists**: every list in the pack scrolls as a window with a hard edge rather than a set of rows that appear and vanish whole. The rows on either side of the visible window are laid out as well, just beyond the edge, and every row is cut by the edge of its own column, so a row leaving with the scroll slides out and disappears the moment it crosses the line, and a row arriving is uncovered from that same line as it comes in. A row cut by the edge takes neither the pointer nor a click on its hidden half. Rows of settings, the side lists of the managers, world and server cards, shop sections and shop tiles all work this way.
*   **Hints**: hold the pointer on a setting and an explanation unfolds from under the cursor in a small glass window of its own. It stands entirely below the row it explains, or above it when there is no room, so it never covers the control being read, and it slides along as the pointer moves across the row. The motion is its own — a short overshoot on arrival and a plain fade on the way out, nothing like the way screens materialise — and because it is drawn inside the same offscreen stage as the rest of the screen it burns away with everything else when the screen closes and folds into the world along with it. The text comes from one lang key per setting, so an explanation is added by writing a line, not by touching a screen; a setting that needs none simply has no line.
*   **Search**: every panel the pack draws for itself carries a search box in its header, to the right of the back button. Typing filters the rows of the open tab as you go, keeping the heading of a group only while something under it still matches, and the panel shrinks to what is left rather than leaving a field of empty glass. The search is not confined to the tab being read: rows that match on the other tabs of the same screen are listed underneath their own tab name, and opening one switches tabs with the query intact. The box has a plate of its own rather than the shared one, so nothing is drawn twice under it; it lifts into place, the lens icon takes the accent as the field is focused, and an accent hairline grows out from the middle of the plate to both ends. Escape clears the query before it closes the screen, unless a row is in the middle of taking typed input of its own. Tabs that lay their rows out by hand instead of through the shared placement - the saved presets page, the create pages of the zone, mark and shop managers - simply do not show the box, because filtering there would promise something the page cannot do.
*   **Announcements**: an operator can put a message in front of chosen players. The recipients are picked as a set rather than one at a time: everyone on the server, any number of scoreboard teams, any number of named players, and a player caught by both a team and a name still sees it once. The message is written into a field that lives as a row of the list, so it scrolls and is searched with everything else, and the delivery is a client request the server checks again on arrival - permission, rate, and the length of the text. It can arrive as a notification in the corner, as a plate in the middle of the screen with the same weight as the final-point unlock, or as both. `/bc announce all|team|player` does the same from chat with full completion of teams and player selectors.
*   **World scan**: Explosion Overhaul indexes the world for lamps, dripstone and glass on the first entry, and its own overlay for that is taken off the bus entirely - prompts, guide pages and the progress readout. What the player sees instead is a card in the pack's own material, placed like any other HUD element and editable in the layout editor: the title, the tally of what has been found, the remaining time as the other mod measures it rather than a timer of ours, and a progress bar that eases towards the real share instead of stepping with it. The card slides in and out with the scan and settles into the accent when the pass completes.
*   **Pickers**: a row that steps through a list of values answers the click itself. The arrow under the pointer grows, the pressed one shrinks and shoves itself a couple of units in the direction of travel, and the value slides out of the box the way the list moved while the new one slides in behind it, clipped to the space between the arrows. The explanation under the row belongs to the value rather than to the row: each option can carry its own text, and when the value changes the old explanation fades out at its own pace instead of being rewritten in place, then the new one arrives after the usual dwell.
*   **Scaling**: the whole HUD compensates for the game's GUI scale, so it keeps the same physical size at scale 1, 2 or 3, and a multiplier on top of that is one of the sliders in the customization screen.

Everything the customization screen touches is stored in `battlecraft/customization.bcfg`, a json document behind an extension of its own. The reader never trusts the file: a value of the wrong type costs its own field and nothing else, a value out of range is clamped as it is read, a key this build does not know is carried through untouched so that a trip back to an older jar does not erase it, and a file that will not parse at all is moved aside as `customization.bcfg.broken-<stamp>` rather than overwritten. Presets keep a snapshot of their own tuning inside the file, so retuning a built-in preset in a later build reaches every player without eating the sliders they moved by hand. What remains in `config/battlecraft-hud.toml` is the rest of the client options: auto-hide toggle and delay, empty-slot collapsing, hotbar slot numbers, glass refraction, interface sound volume, hover cue toggle, the main menu intro, toast notifications, the two heartbeat switches described above, and switches to fall back to vanilla status bars, effect icons or boss bars.

## Modules Included

1.  **Airdrop Mod** (`/airdrop`): Handles scheduled, localized loot drop events with customizable loot tables. An entry is added either by item argument or straight from the hand (`loot hand <min> <max> <percent>`), which carries the whole stack: enchantments, attribute modifiers and anything `/ie` has written. Guns take attachments in place with `loot attach <index> <attachment>`, completed from what the weapon actually accepts — TACZ attachments are checked against the gun's own compatibility rules and SuperbWarfare slots against the levels that gun declares, and a refusal says whether the item is not a gun, the attachment is unknown, or it simply does not fit.
2.  **Capture Points** (`/capturepoint`, `/finalpoint`): Implements team-based spatial capture objective gameplay. While a point is being taken from its owner, every member of the owning team is warned once a second with the time left before the point flips. Regular and final points share the same zone geometry: a circular or square footprint picked at creation or later with `setshape`, sized with `setradius` and bounded vertically with `setheight` / `resetheight`. Points only run while a match is on: in the lobby nothing arms, nothing ticks and the key answers that the match has not started, so a team can no longer walk the objectives — and collect their income and buffs — before the round begins.

    Each point carries its own capture mode, set with `setmode` or from the point manager, and the classic one — a single attacker who presses the key inside the zone — remains the default. An automatic point needs no key at all: five seconds of standing in it arm the capture, the prompt naming the key is not drawn there, and leaving before those five seconds are up drops the arming back to nothing. Relay keeps the single attacker but hands the accumulated progress to a teammate standing in the zone the moment the attacker falls, and picks up a later arrival the same way instead of leaving the point dead. The squad modes make the whole attacking team the attacker: everyone in the zone glows in their team colour, anyone who walks in joins, and the capture only stops once the last of them is off the point. The contest modes open the point to every team at once — the side with the most players on the point drives it, every rival head subtracts from that lead in proportion (four against three moves at a quarter speed), and equal numbers freeze the capture until somebody dies. Both the squad and the contest modes come in a key-started and a walk-in variant.

    Progress is never dropped in one step. A capture that loses its attackers rolls back, and each point carries four percentages for the arithmetic: how fast it is taken, how fast it rolls back when it is left alone, how fast it rolls back while a rival stands on it, and how fast it rolls back once the point already belongs to somebody. The bar has to reach zero before another team's capture starts from there, so a point is always neutralised before it is taken. A player who steps out of the zone keeps a place in the capture for three seconds before being written off, but stops counting as an attacker the moment the edge is crossed: with nobody left standing inside, the bar freezes where it is instead of filling itself during the run back, and the HUD says so. It resumes on return and rolls back once the grace runs out. This holds for all seven modes, they share the same tick. What the block says while all of that happens is two pieces rather than one line: the point and the percentage on the left, and the state — PAUSED, ROLLBACK, STALL, the head count of a contest, the countdown before an arming point starts — as a tag after a separator. The tag is what moves: it rises out and the next one comes up from under it, the card widens to the new word instead of jumping to it, and the percentage ticking underneath changes nothing. They used to share one translated string, which made a state change indistinguishable from a number change and put both on the same frame. The glow that marks whoever is taking a point is a rule now (`capture_glow`) rather than a fixture — it is server-side, since the effect is on the player and every client sees it, so hiding it from one screen was never an option.

    A player who is knocked down counts for nothing while he is on the floor: he is not an attacker, he does not add a head to his side's numbers, and he is dropped from the capture the moment he goes down rather than after the three-second grace. The bar itself outlives the membership: an attacker who walks out alive keeps it on his screen and watches the rollback drain to the end, and it only leaves him when the attempt is over, when he dies, or when he is knocked down.

    Opening a capture puts the initiating team on a cooldown of its own — for that point, or for every point, whichever the point is set to — and it is charged only when the attempt is lost. Joining somebody else's capture never charges it: a team on cooldown can still walk onto a point another team opened, fight for it and take it, and dying there costs it nothing, because the attempt was not theirs to open.

    The reward is split one of three ways, per point: the full reward to everyone who took part, one reward split evenly among them, or one reward split by contribution — time held on the point plus kills taken on it or around it, remembered across death, and paid to nobody at all if the capture fails or another team finishes it. A share owed to a player who is dead or offline waits for him and is handed over when he returns.
    Block protection covers a point's whole footprint and now carries two switches beside it, both in the point manager and under `capturepoint protection`. `breakplaced` lets anyone break what players have put down inside a zone while everything the map itself carries stays sealed: a block placed inside a point is remembered by position and dimension, saved with the points, and forgotten again the moment it is broken. `denyplace` goes the other way and refuses placement inside a zone at all. The two are exclusive, since remembering player blocks means nothing where none can be placed - turning one on drops the other, and the row of the one that lost says why it is dead.

3.  **Combat Timer** (`/kt`): Implements PvP tagging, combat logging prevention, and safety timers.
4.  **Damage Indicator** (`/dmgndctr`): Renders visual markers and damage output tracking.
5.  **Immortality** (`/immortality`): Temporary invulnerability, safety, or spawn protection logic.
6.  **Item Modifiers** (`/ie`): Command suite to dynamically modify or scale item properties. An attribute amount is a fractional figure rather than a whole one: the row in the `/ie` screen steps a tenth at a time and takes a typed value down to a thousandth, so an eighth of a heart or three hundredths of a point of speed is set from the screen instead of only from the command line, and what is sent to the command is the exact figure with its trailing zeros trimmed.
7.  **Kill Reward** (`/killreward`, `/kr`): Grants custom items or currency configurations on player eliminations.
8.  **Knockdown Mod**: Implements a downed state system for players, allowing revives, self-revive injectors, and surrender mechanics. Its block — the bleed-out timer, the revive and surrender prompts, the progress of whichever of them is running — is drawn after every other mod has finished with the frame, so the full-screen blur EnhancedVisuals runs on a dying player no longer smears the one panel he has to read. The rest of the HUD stays under that blur, which is the point of it.
9.  **Minimap**: A custom client-side spatial radar and global map with team marker synchronization, fallback local scanning, spatial dithering, height scanning, offline-persistent caching, and server-driven live terrain updates shared inside a team. An operator can upload an explored map from the Map panel of `/bc` and hand it to everyone, to one team, or to a single player; a map uploaded for everyone is stored with the world and handed to every player who joins later. The same panel loads terrain rather than sharing it: `/bc map load <32..512>` and the Load tab walk the area around the player, and `/bc map cancel` stops a walk that is running. A walk is dropped when the player changes dimension or logs out, and the map panel reports how far it has got. The minimap settings, reached from the map screen, carry the other half of it: any player can pull the server's map down again - the shared one plus his own team's, never an enemy team's - or forget his own copy, which drops the chunks he has and deletes his cache file for that dimension. An operator sees two more rows there: hand his own map to everyone, wipe the shared map of the dimension, or wipe every team's map of it, files included. Each of those arms itself on the first click and acts on the second, since none of them can be undone. A wipe is a server-side one: nobody's client is told to forget what it walked itself. A marker landing on the map is announced by the map itself: the point swells and settles while two rings walk out of it and fade, on the minimap and on the full screen alike, and it does so for a personal marker, a team marker of one's own and a team marker somebody else has just dropped. The flash is driven off the marker moving rather than off the packet, since the server sends the whole list every time and a new marker is otherwise indistinguishable from one that has been standing there all round.
10. **Quarry Mod** (`/quarry`): Automates localized resource extraction.
11. **Sell Mod** (`/sell`): Integrated shop commands for item liquidations. The currency item is chosen with `/sell setcurrency <item>`, which completes over every registered item including modded ones, and prices with `/sell price set|remove`. Both are pushed to clients so the HUD can show a currency chip: the item icon, the balance carried in the inventory and the value of everything sellable currently held, each written as stacks plus remainder (`5×64+32`). A sale floats the gained amount above the chip.
12. **BattleCraft** (`/battlecraft`, aliased to `/bc`): One command tree covers the mini-game, the zones and the shop; `/bc` is a Brigadier redirect onto the same root, so both names resolve to identical nodes, permissions and completions. Implements the complete game lifecycle of the "Battlecraft" mini-game. Features lobby phase, dynamic team selection via custom ESC menu, automated mod control (disabling sell, quarry, and killreward mods in lobby), capture point HUD filtering for spectators, late-joiners warning system, IP-based anti-abuse protection with UUID-only session reconnects, team surrender voting, execution commands on start/stop/surrender, asynchronous Discord RPC presence integration, and the shared HUD layer described above.

    Sides are whatever the scoreboard holds: every team the server has declared shows up as a column on the select screen, sorted by name, two or ten of them, and the column strip measures itself against the window instead of assuming a pair. Membership belongs to the pack alone, so `/team join`, `/team leave` and `/team empty` are refused for anyone below operator level 4 and a player who reconnects during the lobby is dropped from whatever team he carried in, which keeps the roster and the screen in step.

    Every discipline described below is a switch rather than a decision baked into the jar. `/battlecraft rules` opens a screen of them — client restrictions, combat, parkour — and `rules set <rule> <on|off>` does the same from a command; the state lives in `config/battlecraft-rules.json` on the server and travels to every client on join and on change, so turning one off takes effect immediately and needs no restart. The list covers first-person lock, debug keys, advancement, mod-list, resource-pack and voice-group screens, spectator lighting, sights/firing/reloading dropping to a walk, the zoom lock, projectile chunk escort, holstering during parkour, the dodge fire block, sliding and climbing with a gun, the prone-spam stamina penalty, the jump and the dodge while the sights are up, the stamina cost of sprinting, the glow on whoever is taking a capture point, and the enchantment glint, which the server can put out on every client at once.

    Client-side discipline is enforced while connected to a server: the camera is held in first person — the perspective key is swallowed, so no one peeks around a corner in third person — hitboxes, chunk borders and the chunk reload key are refused at the keyboard handler and any state left enabled from single player is cleared each tick; the advancement, mod list and voice-group screens are blocked; resource pack selection is blocked everywhere, since packs are chosen by the launcher rather than in game, and the pack button in the options screen is greyed out rather than silently refusing to open. Blocking the screen is not the whole of it, because a pack switched on before the session is already loaded: on a server with the rule on, every pack the player put in `resourcepacks` is switched off and the resources reloaded once, packs supplied by the launcher, the mods and the server itself are left alone, and the client reports what it removed. Those player packs are recognised by where they come from rather than by their source flag, since the pack carrying every mod's own resources shares that flag, and a pack counts as refused only once it has survived an attempt to remove it — a reload is not instant, and reading the list in the middle of one accused a client that was doing exactly as told. The server writes that report to the log and to the operators online, tells the player in chat what was turned off, and disconnects anyone whose client kept a pack or sent no report within half a minute. The wait is only ever placed on players the guard can answer for: the guard runs on a foreign server, so the host of a single player or open-to-LAN world is never asked for a report and never disconnected for the silence of a watchdog that was never meant to speak at home. The glint rule works the same way from the other end, and it owns the vanilla settings rather than a copy of them: `hasFoil` is answered once for the whole game, so nothing enchanted shines in hand, in the inventory, on the ground or on armour, while glint speed and glint strength are held at zero on the client and their rows in the accessibility screen are greyed out with the reason on them. The values are held in memory rather than written to `options.txt`, and they are put back the moment the rule lifts or the player leaves the world, so a session on a server does not take a setting away for good. Memory alone is not enough, since the game writes that file itself when it closes: a snapshot is kept beside the client's configuration and read back on the next launch, so even a session that ended in a crash returns the player's own glint speed and strength. Editing the file changes nothing, since the values are clamped every tick, and a client that reports the glint still running is disconnected like one that kept a resource pack. Both rules apply in single player too — the rules there belong to the player, who turns them off in `/battlecraft rules` rather than being told the rule does not count at home.

    The configuration files of the pack are checked on join under the same discipline (`check_configs`). The reference is taken from a real client rather than written by hand: `/bc configs snapshot` asks the operator's own game to walk its `config` folder, hash every file and send the list back in batches, and what arrives is stored with the world. The pack's own files are left out of the walk — the interface configuration, the rules and the module switches are written per player and in single player — and anything else that a player is allowed to change is dropped from the reference afterwards with `/bc configs forget <file>`; `/bc configs list` prints what is watched, `/bc configs clear` takes the whole reference off and with it the check, and `/bc configs recheck` asks everybody again. A joining player is sent the list of paths and answers with one hash per path in the same order, which is what keeps the report inside the 32767 bytes a client packet may carry; the list is remembered per player, so a reference edited between the question and the answer never shifts the meaning of a position. A file that differs, a file that is missing, a report that does not match the list and half a minute of silence all end the same way: the operators online and the log are told which files went wrong, and the player is disconnected with them named. The paths are read on the client under the configuration folder alone — a path that climbs out of it is refused — and the host of a single player or open-to-LAN world is never asked, exactly as with the resource pack guard.

    Weapons and movement are tied together across both weapon mods. Sights and reloading both drop the player to a walk: the sprint and dash keys are released, the parkour dash is cancelled in flight and refused a fresh start, and the zoom mod is held down while the sights are up. Keys are read as bindings rather than as fixed codes, so rebinding movement or attack changes nothing, and a key held down through the whole aim resumes on its own once the sights come down. The gun mod steers its own sprint flag every tick, so the sprint is stopped through the switch it exposes rather than by fighting it — otherwise the player kept running behind the scope. The parkour mod is refused ledge climbs, vaults and cliff grabs while the player sprints with a weapon in hand, so a rifle does not vault a wall, and a slide is refused outright with a gun in hand. While any move that needs both hands is running — climbs, vaults, wall runs, rolls, dives, slides — the held item is not drawn at all, in first person or on the player others see, and the fire and aim keys are held down until the move ends. A dodge keeps the weapon visible but still swallows the attack click, so a roll cannot be used to fire from an unhittable pose. If the bridge to the parkour mod ever goes silent mid-move, the hold releases itself after four seconds rather than leaving a player unable to shoot. Toggling prone is metered: three switches inside three seconds are free, and every one after that drains stamina by a share that grows with the spam, so shrinking the hitbox on a hotkey costs what it is worth. Plain sprinting is charged too. The parkour mod bills only its own dash, and a player holding a rifle is refused that dash outright, so running with a weapon cost nothing at all; every sprinting tick is now measured against the dash's own rate — eighty-five per cent of it empty-handed, a hundred and five with a weapon — with whatever the dash has already taken subtracted, so the two never stack. The charge is made on the client because that is where the parkour mod keeps stamina: the server's copy is a mirror the client overwrites every tick, and a deduction made there was never seen by anyone. Both shares are settings — `sprintStaminaDashPercent` and `armedSprintStaminaDashPercent` — and reach the client with the rules. A sprint jump is billed for the whole flight rather than for the few ticks the boots touch the ground: leaving the ground with upward motion opens a leap, and the charge runs until the player lands or drops the sprint, so hopping down a road costs what running it costs, with a weapon in hand as much as without one. Stepping off a ledge is not a leap and is not charged, and swimming, riding, elytra flight and ladders stay outside the count entirely. The jump itself carries a flat toll on top of that, one per cent of the gauge empty-handed and seven tenths of one with a weapon — a rifle already leaves a player short of breath — taken the moment the boots leave the ground and taken in every case, standing still, walking, sprinting or dashing, so bunny-hopping across an island is no longer the cheap way to cross it. Both tolls are settings like the sprint shares, `jumpStaminaPermille` and `armedJumpStaminaPermille`, and the stamina group of the settings tab steps them a tenth of a per cent at a time or takes a typed figure with a decimal point; either one set to zero switches its half off. Death clears the debt — a player who dies winded comes back with a full gauge and the exhaustion lifted, the refill riding the first second after the respawn because the parkour mod hands the new body a stamina object before it knows what its maximum is. Ordnance from either weapon mod carries its own chunk ticket while it flies: the chunk under the shell and the one it is about to enter are forced for the length of the flight and released the moment it lands, dies or ages out, and tickets left by a crash are dropped when the level loads. Rifle rounds are left alone — they are gone within a second and would only crowd out the shells that matter — so a projectile is escorted only while it is explosive or slow enough to reach the ground.

    Adrenaline out of Survival Instinct is worth more than the effects printed on it, and it is no longer free. While a syringe holds - forty-five seconds - stamina recovers twice as fast and every action costs half of what it usually does; an injector runs thirty-five seconds at three quarters more recovery and a quarter off the cost. The recovery half is written as a modifier on the parkour mod's own recovery attribute, so the boost rides the arithmetic the mod already does instead of a second timer topping the gauge up behind its back. There is no attribute for what an action costs, so the discount is refunded rather than deducted: the client watches the gauge fall each tick and gives back the dose's share of the drop with the fractions carried between ticks, which covers everything that spends stamina - the parkour mod's own moves as much as our sprint, jump and prone tolls. The heart goes with it: the trace on the vitals card and the beat in the ears run faster than any wound drives them, and the edges of the screen darken along with them - the same veil exhaustion draws, but breathing with every beat instead of sitting still, while the blur and the low-health overlay stay tied to wounds alone. The third dose inside three minutes is an overdose and is paid for with Poison II for fifteen seconds and Nausea II for ten. Death clears the count, a knockdown does not, so a squad cannot reset the meter by dropping to the floor and being picked up. Both items carry all of it in their tooltip.

    Every wait in the lobby is a visible countdown rather than a guess. The team screen shows the time left before the match starts, the time left before an undecided player is placed automatically, the hold added when somebody joins late, and the cooldown on switching sides. The first pick is free; every switch after it is locked for `teamSwitchCooldownSeconds` (30 by default), and the join buttons carry the remaining seconds while the lock holds. Once the match starts the screen closes itself and the server refuses further picks; a player who connects mid-match gets the screen for `matchJoinChoiceSeconds` (20 by default) and is placed into the smallest team if the timer runs out. Countdowns travel as remaining ticks and are interpolated client-side, so they stay correct no matter how far the server clock is from the player's.

13. **Zones** (`/battlecraft zone`, `/battlecraft shop`): Bases, safe zones and shop zones as one geometry layer, drawn as a translucent volume with a ring baked onto the terrain — both the ring and the foot of the wall are sampled roughly once per block of the perimeter and follow the ground, so a step in the terrain is a step in the boundary rather than a slab hanging in the air, and a crater blown into the ground under a zone is picked up within a few seconds, plus a world label per zone. Zones stay invisible until the match is running, so the lobby is not covered in walls of colour. A label is faded in and out rather than switched — it rises into place as it comes into view and sinks away as it goes — which is what the shop label needed most, since it only stands while the player is inside a base and used to vanish on the frame they stepped out of one. The shop zone opens a browser with sections, subsections, a spinning preview of the selected item and its price both on the tile and under the model; solid models turn on their axis while flat items sway instead, which keeps a sword or an ingot readable rather than vanishing edge-on once a frame. A model is scaled to its own bounds, so a long rifle fits the frame instead of running off it, and a vehicle stored in a deployment container is previewed as the vehicle itself — sized from its entity type and rendered with its own depth pass — rather than as the crate it ships in. The panel also states what the thing needs before it works: a weapon names the round it feeds on, and a vehicle names every gun it carries with the shells each one takes, read out of the mods' own data rather than a table of ours, so a cannon with armour-piercing, high-explosive and canister shells shows all three and an energy weapon says so instead. Above that a vehicle carries its power line — the charge its cell holds in FE and the battery packs that fill it, taken largest first, so a main battle tank reads as two medium packs and a wheelchair as a single cell. Passenger machine-gun stations come out of the data unnamed and by the half-dozen, and their rounds are folded into one line instead of being repeated under six headings. A tile the player cannot afford leaves the buy button dead and states the shortfall instead of sending a purchase the server will refuse. The row of tiles carries the same margin on both sides, the scrollbar is wide enough to be taken hold of and dragged, choosing another tile crossfades the selection instead of switching it on the same frame, and a weapon whose attachments and statistics do not fit the panel is scrolled rather than cut off. The answer to a purchase is written inside the screen, not thrown behind it. A name too long for a tile is set a size down and over two lines rather than run edge to edge across the card, which read as text escaping it; only a name that will not fit even then is scrolled, and its ends fade out instead of being sliced by the tile. A long item name is set over as many lines as it needs inside the panel instead of running off both of its edges, and the whole browser is written in the live ink rather than the muted one, the section and the department the player is standing in included. In the admin screens the notice that no section exists yet is centred over the column of rows and wrapped to it, so it no longer lies across the list beside it. Sections, departments and single items can each be limited to a set of teams, which is how one map carries a separate shop per side: what a team may not buy never reaches its client at all, and the server checks the limit again when the purchase arrives rather than trusting that it was hidden. The whole access map is edited in the admin screen — pick the section, the department or the item, then tick the teams — and the commands underneath it stay available. An item also carries a line of its own writing: `shop item describe <section> <entry> <text>` sets it, the same command without a text takes it off, and the admin screen has a page for it — pick the item, type the line, save it. It is shown at the head of the notes under the model, above whatever the mods themselves say about the thing, which is where a rule of the map ("one per round", "leaves no crate") belongs.

    Each zone carries a set of rules, edited with `edit <id> rule <rule> allow|deny|default` and listed with `rules <id>`. A rule left alone follows the default for the zone's type, so changing the type does not silently discard a deliberate setting, and only the deliberate ones reach `zones.json` — keyed by name, so inserting a rule into the enum never reshuffles what is already on disk. The rules are `invulnerable` and `pvp` (on and off respectively inside a base, scoped to the owning team), plus `block_break`, `block_place`, `explosions`, `mob_spawn`, `interact`, `item_drop` and `hunger`, all permitted everywhere by default. Two more, `owner_block_break` and `owner_block_place`, are exemptions rather than gates: denied by default, and when allowed they let members of the zone's owning team build where `block_break` / `block_place` are otherwise denied, which is how a base is sealed against everyone but the team holding it. An exemption needs an owning team to mean anything, and where zones overlap every zone that denies the action must grant the exemption before it is allowed. Denying `explosions` strips the affected positions and entities out of the blast before it resolves, so a zone survives anything routed through the game's own explosion — TNT, crystals, SuperbWarfare and TACZ ordnance alike — rather than only the hits that arrive as a player breaking a block. Creative players are exempt from the building rules. Rules travel to the client along with the zone, so a screen can read them without a round trip.

    Every command with branches has a screen behind it, and a screen reads live state rather than the snapshot it was built from: a value flips the moment the server confirms it, an arrow cycles all the way back to "default", and the answer to the action — including the refusal — is written on a plate under the panel. A form says what it is still missing (an identifier not entered, a name already taken, no zone picked, no scoreboard team to own one) instead of doing nothing when pressed. Panels lay themselves out twice when the first pass changes their height, so a field or a row cannot end up placed against the previous tab's geometry. Where a list runs past its panel, a chevron breathes at that edge — smaller at the top than at the bottom, lit with the same halo the type carries, and given room of its own so it never sits on a row or a tab. It is one filled outline rather than two strokes meeting: a pair of strokes overlaps where it meets, and every translucent pass lays a second coat of alpha there, which reads as a bead stuck on the tip.

    The shop editor takes attachments too (`shop item attach <section> <entry> <attachment>`), against the same compatibility check, and the airdrop and shop screens both carry the picker for it.

14. **Vision Goggles compatibility**: the goggles mod registers its key bindings during client setup, after Forge has already fired `RegisterKeyMappingsEvent`, so its three keys never reach `options.txt` in a form the game reads back - every launch resets them to N, M and V, and the launcher cannot move them off the keys the voice chat and the SecurityCraft cameras already sit on. The pack registers its own three bindings at the proper time (`goggles_toggle` on N, `goggles_mode` on M, `goggles_zoom` on Y), silences the mod's own ones, and sends the mod's own toggle packet when they are pressed, so the server side of the goggles stays untouched.

    The filter itself was being wiped by another mod. SuperbWarfare's client tick calls `turnOffThermalImaging` on every tick the thermal imager is off, and that method shuts the whole vanilla post-processing chain down without asking whose it was, so the goggles' shader lived for exactly one tick and never reached the screen while the HUD and the night vision effect stayed. That call is redirected: a chain belonging to the goggles is left alone, and only a foreign one is shut down. The check is by chain name rather than by reference, so the order the two mods' tick handlers run in stops mattering, and while SuperbWarfare's own thermal imaging is running the pack does not touch the post effect at all.

    The picture is driven from the server rather than from what the client can find in its own curios inventory. Every tick the server reads the equipped goggles - whether they are on, which mode, how much charge is left, whether a zoom module is fitted - and sends that to their owner when it changes. The client keeps the mod's own post-processing chain loaded off that state, restores it when a resource reload or another mod drops it, holds `effectActive` on so a stray F4 cannot switch the visor off, and feeds the shader its `time`, `battery`, `colorFilter` and `focus` uniforms every frame. The mod's own HUD modules are emptied out and the charge and the mode are drawn instead as one more card in the pack's own material, next to the status card in the bottom right: the charge as a large figure with its own bar underneath, the mode set small at the top and swapped by rising out of place rather than by switching, the whole card sliding in and out and pulsing red once the battery runs low. It is placed, tinted and switched off in the interface editor like every other block.

15. **Shared Utilities** (`com.persiki84.shared`): Single source of cross-cutting helpers (`CommandHelper`, `ConfigHelper`, `ModMessage`, `WorldFiles`) plus the client UI layer (`UiTheme`, `UiRender`, `UiGlass`, `UiBackdrop`, `UiSound`, `UiAnim`, `Smooth`, `UiButton`, `UiSlider`). All modules consume these directly; per-module copies are not permitted.

## Conventions

*   **Localization**: All player-facing text is emitted via `Component.translatable` against per-module `assets/<modid>/lang/{en_us,ru_ru}.json`. Raw `Component.literal` with embedded display strings is disallowed. Config-file comments, log records, and Discord Rich Presence are exempt as they bypass the in-game language pipeline.
*   **Render colors**: The palette lives in `UiTheme`; module-specific semantic names alias it in per-module color holders (e.g. `CaptureColors`). One-off render values remain inline.
*   **Accent**: the launcher writes its accent next to the instance and `UiAccent` is the sole holder of it. A colour too dark to read on glass is lifted in OKLab, where lightness is separate from hue and chroma, so a deep red stays red instead of going pink the way a mix toward white takes it. Fills are lifted to a lightness of 0.56 and text to 0.70, since one threshold either bleached the fills or left small type unreadable, and an accent already above its threshold reaches the interface byte for byte as the launcher set it. Saturation travels with lightness rather than staying put: chroma is scaled in the same proportion as the lift, since a colour raised at constant chroma washes out and one lowered at constant chroma thickens, which is what used to leave the dimmed ink looking deeper in hue than the main one when the whole of it should differ by brightness alone. A colour picked by hand in the customization window is lifted the same way, so one hex value gives one colour wherever it was entered, and every row of the colours tab reads back the ink that is actually being drawn rather than the factory constant behind it.
*   **Animation**: Timed values go through `Smooth`/`UiAnim.approach` with the per-frame delta from `UiFrame` — never raw per-frame constants.
*   **Configuration**: Static settings use `ForgeConfigSpec`; dynamic runtime-mutable state (e.g. SellMod price maps) is persisted as JSON.

## Installation and Development

To compile and build the server pack:

```bash
# Clone the repository and navigate to the project directory
cd BattleCraft-mod

# Compile the codebase
./gradlew compileJava

# Build the final JAR
./gradlew build
```

The output JAR will be located under `build/libs/`.

## Map and first-start fixes

Map uploads use batches of at most 30 chunks to stay below Minecraft's serverbound payload limit. Both map sharing interfaces use this limit. Region textures are created when visible and restored from stored chunk colors after eviction, so loading another area no longer blanks previously explored regions.

Loading a client chunk no longer deletes the same chunk from the persistent map. Previously the stale GPU image hid that loss until a region was evicted, producing black holes after fast travel or server-side map loading; reconnecting only appeared to fix it because the server sent the missing chunks again. The normal rolling rescan still refreshes terrain that actually changed. Cancelling a server-side map scan now flushes its last partial batch, so every chunk already processed remains on the client and in the team map.

Operators can middle-click the full map to teleport. The server checks the permission, request rate, coordinate range and world border, then uses the higher of the player's current Y and the target surface: a player below the terrain lands above it, while a player already flying higher keeps that altitude. Capture-point indicators in the HUD use their full team or accent colour; map markers keep their separate subdued treatment.

The vanilla unverified-chat warning toast is filtered by its exact system-toast token. Other system notifications and the underlying chat behaviour are unchanged.

BattleCraft skips Minecraft's accessibility onboarding and opens its own setup or main menu. Font samples fit their card width, and the setup panel scales to fit short windows, including GUI scale 4. Accessibility settings remain available from the game options.

## Interface pass, 12 September 2026

World markers and zone prompts now leave the screen while the player list is held open, and they leave by fading rather than by being dropped from the frame. The key is read from the player's own binding through one shared check (`UiHud.rosterOpen`) instead of a hard-coded Tab, so rebinding the list moves the behaviour with it; the capture HUD, which already hid itself there, was moved onto the same check.

Value pickers carry per-value explanations. A picker looks for `<option key>.hint` and falls back to the row's own hint, so the seven capture modes, the three reward splits and the two cooldown scopes each explain themselves. The hint window treats a changed text on the same row as a departure: it fades the old wording out and brings the new one in after the usual dwell, instead of swapping the words under the cursor.

Search is available on every panel built on the shared manager screen, filtering the open tab and listing matches from the others underneath their tab names. Text rows can now live in the list itself (`FieldRow`), which is what the announcement message uses; fields that draw their own plate are marked as such so the shared field pass does not draw a second one under them.

Announcements from an operator reach a chosen set of players, teams or everyone, as a corner notification, a centre plate, or both.

The search box sits at the left edge of the panel and the back button moved to the right, so the header lines up with the panel it belongs to. Neither field carries an accent underline any more - focus is shown the way every other field in the pack shows it, with a rim and an inner light.

Returning from a sub-panel is animated: the panel leaves by the player's own motion set and the screen behind it plays its entrance again rather than being swapped in whole. Escape still closes the menu outright; only the back button returns.

Announcements carry their own duration, from two to sixty seconds, set in the panel or as an argument of the command, and it governs both the centre plate and the corner notification. The scan card now hides itself once the foreign mod's own hide time passes - that rule lived in the overlay that was taken off the bus, so without it the finished card stayed up for the rest of the session. Notification cards no longer hold a 96-unit floor, and the slot box is measured off the widest card rather than the ceiling, so a short message gets a short card and the editor outline matches what is drawn. Rings are subdivided by a finer arc step, which is what the magnifier in the search box needed to stop reading as a polygon.

Every row of the `/bc` hub now has an explanation - match actions, the status lines, all twenty lobby and stamina settings, and each entry of the manage tab. Until now only the module switches had them, which is why hovering the other tabs looked as though hints were broken.

## Shop, modifiers and map, 12 September 2026 (second pass)

The shop admin panel gained a Layout tab. It picks the section itself, any of its subsections or any
of its items and moves the chosen one up or down, drops it, or - for an item - moves it between the
subsections of that section. Adding a held item now takes a target subsection instead of always
landing in the section root, and `item id` accepts one too. The same operations exist as commands:
`section order`, `subsection order` and `item order` with `up` or `down`, `subsection move` to another
section, and `item move` to another section or subsection.

An item is looked up by identifier inside a whole section, subsections included, so two items sharing
an identifier made one of them unreachable. New and moved items now take the first identifier free in
the destination section tree, and a subsection carried into another section renames only the items
that would collide.

The shop grid counted one row less than it could fit, because it charged a gap to every row instead of
to the gaps between them. The missing row was still painted - the clipping band covered the whole
panel rather than the tiles - so the last row was visible but never took a click. The band now equals
the tile area, and scrolling moves whole rows: the previous clamp stopped at the last item instead of
the last row, which shifted every tile into a neighbouring column and left the scrollbar thumb short
of the bottom. The thumb is measured in rows as well. Switching a section or a subsection slides the
tiles in from the side the new section lies on, and scrolling no longer replays the entrance
animation. In the sidebar the clipping band started at the panel edge while the rows started below the
scroll hint, which left the spare row above the list standing as a cut plate at the top.

Item modifiers work in percent when the operation multiplies: both multiplying operations take a
share, so the value row switches to percent with the operation and the stored multiplier stays the
same number. Fractional values accept a comma as well as a dot - the numeric keypad on a Russian
layout sends one, and the row used to drop it silently, turning 0,5 into 5. Amounts are formatted
without a locale, and the shop card no longer prints a multiplier as if it were a percentage. The menu
can now write either into the held stack or into the config entry for that item kind, which is what
the commands always could: a modifier written into the stack travels with that one item, a modifier
written into the config reaches every item of the kind, shop purchases included. Modifiers stored in
an item's NBT keep a stable identity derived from the attribute and the slot instead of the position
in the list, which shifted whenever a neighbouring entry was removed.

The client painted map chunks within eight chunks of the player regardless of render distance, so
anything loaded further out stayed black until the player walked over it, and a fast trip left holes
behind. The paint window now follows the effective render distance, capped at the reach the server
accepts, and is swept from the centre outwards with a time budget rather than a fixed count of chunks.
Painted chunks are sent to the team in batches twice a second instead of one packet per chunk. A paint
that comes out empty is no longer stored: it means the chunk had nothing to paint, and keeping it
would have frozen a black square into the map forever, since a known chunk is not painted again.

## Module panels, 12 September 2026 (third pass)

The airdrop panel offered a warning time up to a day while the command takes an hour, so anything
above that was rejected without a word; the row now stops where the command does. Loot entries were
add-and-delete only, and everything else the loot commands can do had to be typed. An Entry tab now
picks a stored entry and edits its count range and chance, moves it up or down the table, duplicates
it, strips the item tags off it or removes it, and the Loot tab can empty the table in one go. The
count rows keep the range valid on their own, so lowering a maximum below the minimum drags the
minimum with it instead of failing. Writing raw NBT into an entry stays a command: the argument is a
compound tag, and a text field that rejects most of what is typed into it is worse than no field.

The quarry panel can set the cooldown of the block in the crosshair, not only the global one, and a
List tab shows the registered blocks with their position, dimension and any custom cooldown. The list
in the snapshot is capped and the panel says how many blocks it did not show, because the snapshot
travels whole on every request and these blocks run into the thousands.

Immortality gained a Players tab: every player online is a row, the switch grants or takes immortality
away, and a granted row carries the seconds left. Granting is refused while the module is off and
turning it off clears the list, so the rows are locked rather than silently doing nothing.

Item modifiers can write a lore line onto the held item and clear its lore. The item chosen on the
Items tab is now a target of its own: the effect and attribute tabs used to act on the held item or on
its kind whatever was selected in the list, so picking an item there changed nothing. Picking one now
switches the target to it, and the target row spells out where the next addition lands.

Selling prices could only be pinned to the held item although the command takes an identifier. A
By id tab takes one, checks it against the item registry before sending anything, and sets the price
or makes the item the currency.

## Capture points, marks and command coverage, 13 September 2026

A capture point can run a console command when it is taken. The command is stored with the point, runs
from the server console at the point's own position, and takes `%point%`, `%team%`, `%x%`, `%y%` and
`%z%`. It is set with `capturepoint setcommand <point> <command>` or from the Bonuses tab of the point
panel, and removed with `clearcommand` or the button next to the field.

The capture reward can be switched off from the panel: its amount accepts zero, which clears the
reward the way `removereward` always did, and the reward and income items are taken from the hand
instead of being typeable only as a command argument. Income, its interval, the owner effect and the
capture command now come from one branch builder, so a final point carries the same set as an ordinary
one - the panel used to send `finalpoint setincome`, a command that did not exist.

The owner of a point can be cleared. `capturepoint clearowner <point>` and `finalpoint clearowner
<point>` release the point and reset its cooldown, and the owner row carries a `none` entry that sends
the same command.

A mark can be taken off the world while staying on the map: the world tag fades out the way every
other tag does and the mark keeps its place on the minimap and the full map. Marks can also be renamed
from the panel, which until now was only possible at creation.

Capture point tags in the world leave with the same animation as base, shop and mark tags when the
player list is held open. The capture HUD dropped out of the frame instead, which read as a defect
next to the tags that faded.

A zone carries a colour row again - eight presets plus the team colour, with a colour set by command
shown as its own entry instead of being reported as the first preset - and `zone edit <id> clearcolor`
puts a zone back onto its team colour. The spawn anchor row checks that the operator stands inside the
zone before sending, because the command refuses otherwise. The shop admin panel can add an item by
identifier instead of only from the hand, and the Layout tab moves a subsection or an item into
another section.

World tags carry a size of their own. The slider sits in the interface tab of the customization
screen, runs from half to one and a half of the stock size and covers the labels of bases, shops,
capture points and operator marks, the range readout beside them and the plain dots of player markers.
It travels with presets and with the config like every other interface dial.

The whole capture HUD leaves with the player list rather than only its tags: the objective bar, the
return card, the point pills and the centre progress card fade out and come back instead of being
dropped from the frame, and the victory banner rides the same fade. A pill that fades out under the
list keeps its place in the set, so it does not come back as a new pill without its owner history.

Panels refresh from the data rather than from the number of rows. A rename, a colour, a captured point
or a command added to a list changes nothing about how many rows a screen has, so screens that
compared counts did not notice: the hub, the point panel, the mark panel and the zone panel now
compare a signature of what they show, the way the shop layout tab already did. Adding or removing a
match command now appears in the list at once, and the command and effect fields of a point follow the
point when its stored value changes elsewhere.

In the `/bc` hub the Require teams switch sent `config requireTeams`, a path that was never
registered, and did nothing; it sends `config set requireTeams` now. The console commands run at match
start, at match stop and on surrender have a tab of their own: every entry is a row that removes
itself, and each of the three lists has a field and an Add row. Clearing the late-join grace has a row
of its own rather than only appearing as an unmet checklist item, game rules can be reset from the
rules panel, and a team can be created under any name instead of the four suggested ones. The build
config check - snapshot, recheck, clear and forgetting a single path - has a panel instead of living
only in chat.

## Shop stock, map labels, point roles and the IFF device, 14 September 2026

A shop item chooses whose stock a purchase spends. Shared keeps one count for the whole server, the
way it always worked; team gives every team its own count and its own restock timer; personal gives
every player theirs. A pickaxe limited to one every thirty seconds no longer queues the whole team
behind one man, and nothing has to be left unlimited to avoid it. The setting is a row on the Stock
tab of the shop panel and the command `shop item scope <section> <item> shared|team|player`. Changing
the scope returns everyone to a full stock, because a personal remainder cannot be handed to a team.
A purchase from a personal or team stock is now sent back only to the people it concerns instead of
the whole catalogue going out to everybody.

The map carries labels: text without a plate, without a dot, glowing in its own colour. The middle
mouse button opens a menu of actions on the point it was pressed at, so teleport has company instead
of being the only thing that button does: teleport here, a new label, and on an existing label - edit
the text, add a line, drop the last line, change the colour and delete it. Lines are added up to six;
the first line is the caption the mark panel already edits. An operator moves a label by double
clicking it and dragging; a single click keeps placing a personal marker as before. Hovering a label
raises a soft aura in its colour, an armed label breathes, and both the action menu and the text box
arrive with an animation rather than appearing whole. Labels obey the same team visibility as marks,
so a line can be shown to one team and hidden from the rest, and they sit in the mark panel with a
kind row and the line list.

A capture point is required or optional. The final point opens once one team holds every required
point; an optional one is taken for income, bonuses or a command and nobody waits for it. If a map
carries no required points at all, the final one is open from the start - there is nothing to fulfil.
A point can also be hidden from the HUD while staying on the map: it drops out of the objective bar
and out of the world tags but is captured as usual. Both are rows on the point tab and the commands
`setrequired` and `sethud` of either root. A new switch decides whether the final point belongs to the
team that opened it: with it on, the side that collected every required point cannot have its win
taken by a rival who walks onto the final point it just unlocked.

While a match runs every player carries the SuperbWarfare IFF device in its Curios slot. It cannot be
dropped, cannot be taken out of the slot - anything found in the inventory goes back within a second -
and it is removed when the match ends or the rule is turned off. The rule lives with the other combat
rules; without SuperbWarfare or Curios on the server the row says so instead of pretending to work,
and nothing is issued.

The vanilla `/clear` walks the inventory and the crafting grid only, so anything worn in a Curios slot
survived it. It now clears those slots too, with the same count and the same `maxCount` limit, and
with a count-only run when the limit is zero.

A capture point holds a list of commands, not one. Every command is a row of the Bonuses tab showing
its full text with a Remove button of its own, the field below only adds a new one, and a point takes
up to eight. `addcommand` and `removecommand <n>` join `setcommand`, which still replaces the whole
list the way it always did, and a point saved with the old single command keeps it as the first line.

Map labels carry their own size. It does not follow the map zoom - a label reads the same at any
scale - and it is changed either by the Size row of the mark panel, by `mark edit <id> scale <percent>`,
or with the mouse the way the HUD editor works: arm the label with a double click and drag the corner
handle that appears. The action menu on a label also sets who sees it - everyone, or a chosen set of
teams - and the palette gained black.

An operator now receives every mark, including those hidden from their own team. Without that a label
hidden from the operator's team vanished from their own map and could no longer be moved or restored.

The shop has a showcase editor. The Layout tab opens the shop the way a player sees it, on the whole
catalogue rather than the part one team is offered: tiles are dragged to reorder and the neighbours
glide out of the way, the buy button becomes a remove button, and the item model spins and zooms as
usual. A drop sends one command for the final position instead of a run of single steps.

Switching a value with the arrows animates again. The animation was always there, but every edit
rebuilt the screen and replaced the row with a fresh one whose animation had already finished - the
better the panel updater got, the more it looked like nothing moved. A row now hands its animation to
its replacement. Deleting no longer throws the selection back to the top of the list either: it steps
to the neighbour, so removing several things in a row does not mean scrolling back every time.

Everything on the full map keeps its size relative to the map rather than to the screen. A marker of a
fixed screen size looks smaller against terrain that grew under a zoom and bloated over terrain that
shrank, which reads as the marker adjusting itself. Bases, shops, player and team markers, capture
points, the player arrow, every caption and the labels now follow the zoom, clamped at both ends so
nothing disappears at the lowest zoom or floods the screen at the highest. The minimap is untouched:
its zoom is a setting rather than a gesture.

The showcase editor can look through any team's eyes. Sections, subsections and items are restricted
per team, so the header carries a button that switches the whole showcase between the full catalogue
and what one chosen team is actually offered. The editor also stays open outside a shop zone - it is
opened from the panel, and leaving the zone used to close it in the same tick.

Markers follow the zoom along a square root rather than in direct proportion, with a floor: direct
proportion ate a marker down to a dot once the map was zoomed out.
